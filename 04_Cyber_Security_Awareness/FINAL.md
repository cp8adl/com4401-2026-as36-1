2. Multi-factor (MFA) and Two-factor (2FA)
Authentication.

‘Something you know’

(PSharma,CyberSecurity, 2025.) The ‘something you know’ factor refers to an
authentication process which relies on information a user can remember, for example, a
password, passphrase or a personal identification number (PIN). In multi-factor
authentication (MFA), this factor can be combined with other forms of verification, such as
something a unique physical characteristic or something a person possesses, to provide
an additional layer of security. Two-factor authentication (2FA) is a type of MFA which
requires two different authentication factors to verify a user’s identity.

‘Something you have’

(National Cyber Security Centre, 2026). The something you have authentication factor
refers to a physical or digital credential that a user possesses to verify their identity. In
FIDO2 authentication, this factor is represented by a private, cryptographic key stored
within an authenticator, such as a mobile device or a physical security key. When
combined with user verification through a PIN, biometric feature or a password, this
provides multiple layers of authentication and helps protect accounts from unauthorised
access.

‘Something you are’

(National Cyber Security Centre, 2026) The ‘something you are’ authentication factor
refers to a person’s unique biological characteristics, such as their facial features or
fingerprint, which can be used to verify their identity. In multi-factor authentication (MFA),
biometric recognition can be combined with another authentication factor, such as a
passkey stored on a device. For example, a user may need to verify their identity using
facial recognition or a fingerprint before their device allows them to use a passkey to
access an online account. This combines something you have (cryptographic credential or
the device) with something you are (the user’s biometric information), providing two
distinct authentication factors. However, biometric authentication does not eliminate
every security risk. Devices can still be compromised, and the security of the
authentication process depends on how the device, and its authentication systems are
implemented and protected. Therefore, organisations should consider both the benefits
and potential vulnerabilities of biometric authentication when choosing suitable security
measures.

Weaknesses of Traditional Multi-Factor Authentication.

(National Cyber Security Centre, n.d.) Traditional MFA methods, such as authenticator
applications, SMS verification codes and hardware-generated one-time passwords (OTPs),
give an additional layer of security beyond passwords. However, these methods have
certain vulnerabilities and do not offer the same level of protection against modern
cyberattacks. A key weakness is that traditional MFA methods remain susceptible to
phishing, particularly adversary-in-the-middle (AiTM) attacks, where attackers use
fraudulent websites to relay authentication information to legitimate services in real time

(National Cyber Security Centre, n.d) SMS-based authentication is vulnerable to SIM-
swapping attacks, where criminals transfer and victims telephone number to a SIM card
under their control, allowing them to receive verification codes intended for the legitimate
user. SMS codes may also be intercepted by certain types of malware, particularly on
mobile devices. Consequently, although SMS verification can reduce the risks associated
with password theft, it does not provide strong protection against attackers who can obtain
or relay the additional authentication code.

Hardware OTP tokens provide another authentication option by generating codes using a
physical device. They can reduce certain risks associated with software-based credential
storage and malware, particularly when the token is separate from the user's computer.
However, hardware-generated OTPs remain vulnerable to phishing and AiTM attacks
because the codes are not cryptographically bound to the legitimate website. Attackers
can therefore relay a valid code to the genuine service during its short validity period.

Overall, traditional MFA methods improve account security compared with password-only
authentication, but they do not eliminate the risk of account compromise. The National
Cyber Security Centre’s comparison identifies phishing- resistant FiDO2 credentials,
including passkeys and security keys, as offering stronger protection against common
credential attacks because authentication is cryptographically linked to the legitimate
website's domain. However, their security still depends on factors such as device
protection, secure credential storage, and account recovery arrangements.

The effectiveness of multi-factor authentication in preventing account
takeover.

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) is an effective security measure for reducing the
likelihood of unauthorised account access. Meyer et al. (2023) investigated its
effectiveness using a large dataset of Microsoft Azure Directory accounts exhibiting
suspicious activity. Their findings indicated that MFA reduced the estimated risk of
account compromise by 99.22% across the population. Furthermore, among accounts
associated with leaked credentials, MFA reduced the estimated risk of compromise by
98.56%. These results demonstrate that requiring an additional authentication factor can
provide substantial protection even when passwords have been exposed.

The study also identified differences in the effectiveness of authentication methods.
Dedicated authenticator applications, such as Microsoft Authenticator, provided stronger
protection that SMS-based verification, although both methods offered considerable
greater security than relying on passwords without MFA. Meyer et al. (2023). This suggests
that organisations should consider not only whether MFA is enabled, but also which
authentication method is deployed.

Further evidence is provided by Doerfler et al. (2019), who evaluated fourteen login
challenges against more than 350,000 real world account-hijacking attempts. Their
findings showed that device-based challenges blocked over 94% of phishing-related
attempts and 100% of automated attempts in the dataset. However, these results relate to
particular device-based challenges rather than every MFA method. Overall, the studies
indicate that additional authentication measures can substantially reduce account-
takeover risks, although their effectiveness varies according to the authentication and the
attack technique involved.

Using 2fa over nothing at all.

(NHS England, n.d) Although SMS-based two-factor authentication (2FA) has recognised
security weaknesses, it can still provide greater protection than relying on passwords
alone. NHS England acknowledges that SMS authentication is vulnerable to phishing,
number-porting attacks, SIM-swapping, and therefore recommends using it only when
more secure alternatives and unavailable. Nevertheless, the organisation recognises that
SMS-based authentication can offer an additional layer of protection in comparison to
having no multi-factor authentication in place. Where SMS must be used, organisations
should understand the associated security and privacy risks, implement appropriate
monitoring and develop plans to transition to stronger authentication methods.

CYBERSECURITY AWARENESS: PUBLIC Wi-Fi SECURITY

 Introduction to Public Wi-Fi Security

Public Wi-Fi refers to wireless internet connections available in public places such as
airports, hotels, cafés, libraries, and universities. These networks make internet access

convenient and affordable, but they may also introduce cybersecurity risks when
connections are poorly configured, or users connect to fraudulent hotspots. Public Wi-Fi
security is therefore an important part of cybersecurity awareness for students, traveler's
and employees. Modern website encryption makes many online activities safer than in the
past; nevertheless, users must still recognize deceptive networks, phishing pages, and
device vulnerabilities (FTC, 2023).

1.1 Cybersecurity Threats Associated with Public Wi-Fi

1.1.1 Man-in-the-Middle Attacks

A man-in-the-middle (MITM) attack occurs when an attacker intercepts or manipulates
communications between a device and an online service. On an insecure network, an
attacker may attempt to observe traffic or redirect a user to a malicious destination.
Properly implemented HTTPS encryption helps prevent attackers from reading or altering
the content of website communications, although it does not make every website
trustworthy (FTC, 2023).

1.1.2 Evil Twin Attacks

An evil twin attack involves setting up a fraudulent wireless hotspot that imitates a
legitimate network. For example, an attacker might create a network called
“Airport_Free_WiFi” to persuade travelersto connect. A fake network can present
misleading login pages or attempt to interfere with network traffic. Peer-reviewed research
by Nakhila et al. (2018) examines techniques for detecting such impersonation, illustrating
that rogue access points pose a genuine technical security challenge.

1.1.3 Packet Sniffing and Eavesdropping

Packet sniffing is the capture and inspection of data traveling across a network. Network
administrators use it legitimately for troubleshooting, but malicious actors may exploit
unencrypted traffic to obtain information. The risk depends on the network configuration
and the encryption used by applications. Users should therefore avoid sending sensitive
information through services that do not provide secure connections (CISA, n.d.).

1.1.4 Malware and Phishing

Fraudulent captive portals, malicious websites and deceptive messages can trick users into
revealing credentials or installing unwanted software. Attackers may exploit a user's
assumption that a Wi-Fi login page is legitimate. HTTPS indicates that a connection is
encrypted, not that the website itself is honest; checking addresses and treating
unexpected login requests with suspicion remain essential (FTC, 2023).

1.2 Consequences of Insecure Public Wi-Fi Usage

Compromised accounts can result in identity theft, financial fraud, and loss of personal
information. For organizations, a stolen employee password or infected device may expose
customer records and internal systems, causing business disruption, reputational harm,
and possible compliance problems. Risks increase when workers access sensitive resources
through untrusted connections without appropriate safeguards. Organizational guidance
therefore emphasizes secure remote access, staff training, and sensible access controls
(FTC, 2019).

1.3 Preventive Measures and Best Practices

First, users should verify the network name with staff before connecting and avoid
lookalike hotspots. Second, they should check that websites use HTTPS and should never
ignore certificate warnings. Third, a reputable virtual private network (VPN) can encrypt
traffic between a device and its VPN provider, adding protection on untrusted local
networks. However, VPNs do not eliminate phishing or malware risks, and choose a
trustworthy provider for matters (FTC, 2018). Fourth, multi-factor authentication (MFA)
should be enabled for important accounts, because an additional verification step can limit
damage if a password is stolen. Fifth, devices, browsers, and apps should be updated to
address known vulnerabilities. Users should also disable  automatic connection to
unfamiliar hotspots and turn off unnecessary sharing features. Finally, mobile data or a
personal hotspot may be preferable for sensitive tasks when the legitimacy or security of a
public connection is uncertain (NCSC, 2018; FTC, 2023).

1.4 The Importance of Cybersecurity Awareness and Education

Technical security measures are most effective when supported by informed user behavior.
People may connect to an unknown hotspot for convenience, overlook security warnings or
enter passwords into a convincing fake portal. Cybersecurity education should therefore

teach network verification, phishing recognition, secure browsing, MFA and incident
reporting. Universities and employers can reinforce these habits through short training
sessions, practical demonstrations, and clear remote-working policies. Research on evil
twin detection shows the value of technical controls, while awareness helps users make
safer decisions before an attack occurs (Nakhila et al., 2018).

1.5 Conclusion

Public Wi-Fi provides valuable connectivity, but fraudulent access points, interception
attempts, phishing and insecure applications can create cybersecurity threats. The
potential consequences include compromised accounts, financial loss, and exposure of
organizational information. Public Wi-Fi is not inherently unsafe: HTTPS, appropriate VPN
use, MFA, software updates, and verified network selection can substantially reduce risk.
Consistent cybersecurity education is essential for translating these safeguards into
everyday behavior.

Phishing and Social Engineering

4.1 Understanding Phishing

Phishing

Is an online fraud where criminals pretend to be legitimate and/or reputable companies, friends, or
acquaintances by using fake emails, text messages, phone calls, or websites to trick people into sharing
personal details, passwords, or bank information. They may also send links that can install malware,
harmful software, on your device or steal your information, which can also sabotage systems and
organisations. (Microsoft, n.d.)

Spear phishing

Is a type of online swindle aimed at a particular person or organisation. Criminals use information they
find online, such as someone’s job, workplace, or recent activities, to make fake messages seem real.
Unlike ordinary phishing emails, which often use general greetings like “Dear customer,” these messages
contain details about the person being targeted. The sender may pretend to be a manager or colleague
to gain the victim’s trust. The aim is to steal passwords or money, obtain confidential information, or
persuade the person to open a dangerous link or attachment. (Microsoft, 2026)

Smishing

Is a fraud carried out through text messages. The word comes from combining SMS and phishing.
Scammers send messages that appear to come from a bank or another trusted organisation to persuade
people to share personal details, provide bank information or make payments. The messages may have
links to fake websites and often create a sense of urgency to encourage people to act without checking
if the message is genuine. Scammers may use unknown numbers or copy an organisation’s name to
make the message look real. Their aim is to steal money or obtain private information. (HSBC UK, n.d.)

Vishing

Is a phone fraud in which criminals pretend to be a bank or another trusted organisation to obtain
people’s private information. They may ask for passwords, bank details or credit card numbers over the
phone or through recorded voice messages. To make their calls sound believable, they may use
automated voices or details collected from the internet or previous data breaches. Some scammers also
send emails asking people to ring a number, then use the call to persuade them to reveal personal
details or send money. Sharing this information can result in monetary loss or identity theft, so it is
important to check who is calling before giving out any details. (Barclaycard, n.d.)

Business Email Compromise (BEC)

Is a type of fraud in which criminals impersonate a trusted person, such as a manager or supplier, to
persuade a business or individual to transfer money or disclose confidential information. They may use a
false email address that closely resembles the genuine one or gain access to an existing account by
stealing its password or using malicious software. Fraudsters often make their requests appear urgent to
prevent the recipient from checking whether they are genuine. These scams can cause considerable
financial losses, particularly for small businesses. (HSBC UK, n.d.)

Why Phishing Works

Cyber criminals often exploit human behavior by creating fear, putting people under pressure,
or pretending to be someone they trust. For example, they may pretend to be a manager or
bank employee and use convincing emails or phone calls to obtain confidential information or
persuade someone to transfer money. These messages can be difficult to recognise because
they may look genuine. Staff can also fall for such scams, even after receiving cyber security
training, particularly when they are busy or under pressure at work. Spear-phishing and whaling
target specific individuals and can lead to stolen information, financial losses and disruption to
an organisation. (Bhardwaj et al., 2003)

 Common Warning Signs

Recognised indicators of a phishing message include spelling mistakes, grammatical errors and vague
greetings, such as ‘Dear customer’. The sender’s email address may also hold unusual wording or slight
misspellings of a genuine company name. Other warning signs include suspicious links, unexpected
attachments and requests for passwords or bank details. Some messages create a sense of urgency by
claiming that an account has a problem or threatening a penalty if the recipient does not act
immediately. Scammers may also use personal information or pretend to be someone the recipient
knows to make the message appear genuine. It is important to check the sender’s details and verify the
message before clicking on links, opening attachments or sharing personal information.

 Responding to Phishing

If someone suspects they have received or clicked on a phishing message, they should stop interacting
with it and report it to their organisation’s IT department if it is work-related. If they have entered their
password, they should change it at once and enable multi-factor authentication. If they have shared
bank details or sent money, they should contact their bank straight away. Within an organisation, the IT
team should investigate the incident, check for unauthorised access and prevent further messages from
the attacker where possible. They should also contact the bank promptly if a payment has been made,
to see whether it can be stopped or recovered. Recording what happened and reviewing security
procedures can help the organisation deal with the incident and reduce the risk of a similar attack in the
